    The four principles

    Evidence: strong

    Natural surveillance

    Arrange a place so people can be seen going about it: light, windows and clear lines of sight put eyes on the street without a single camera.

    Evidence: moderate

    Natural access control

    Paths, gates and landscaping guide people where you want them, and make it obvious when someone is somewhere they shouldn’t be.

    Evidence: weak, untested

    Territorial reinforcement

    Clear boundaries between public, shared and private space tell everyone a place belongs to someone.

    Evidence: moderate

    Maintenance

    Broken lights and graffiti signal that nobody is watching. Quick repairs signal the opposite, and keep the other measures working.

    Scientific review

    Does CPTED actually work? What the evidence says

    Crime Prevention Through Environmental Design is one of the oldest ideas in security practice, and one of the most often asserted. This is a review of what the research supports, what it doesn’t, and what that means for the model above.

    Where the idea comes from

    The term was coined by the criminologist C. Ray Jeffery in 1971, and the architect Oscar Newman’s Defensible Space (1972) gave it its best-known form: residents defend space they can see and feel responsible for. It now sits on three better-tested theories. Routine activity theory (Cohen & Felson, 1979) says crime needs a motivated offender, a suitable target and the absence of a capable guardian, all in the same place and time. Rational choice (Cornish & Clarke, 1986) says offenders weigh effort, risk and reward. Situational crime prevention (Clarke) turns both into practice: raise the effort, raise the risk, cut the reward. CPTED is the design end of that toolkit. Each principle on this page works by changing one of those variables.

    How to read the evidence

    Most CPTED evaluations are before-and-after studies, ideally with a comparison area. Those are vulnerable to regression to the mean (schemes go where crime has just peaked), to other things changing at the same time, and to publication bias. A few interventions now have randomised trials or systematic reviews with meta-analysis, and those carry far more weight. The grades below follow that ladder.

    Doesn’t crime just move round the corner?

    This is the most common objection, and the evidence mostly answers it. Guerette and Bowers (2009) reviewed 102 evaluations of situational prevention, covering 574 observations. Displacement showed up in 26% of observations, while a diffusion of benefits (crime falling in the surrounding area as well) showed up in 27%. Where displacement did happen, it was smaller than the reduction it came from. Offenders are not hydraulic: when a place gets harder, many of them simply don’t offend there, and some overestimate how far the measure reaches.

    Three surprises in the evidence

    1. Lighting works in daylight. If lighting only worked by letting people see offenders, night-time crime should fall more than daytime crime. It doesn’t (Welsh & Farrington, 2008). That points to a second mechanism: visible investment signals that the place is cared for and watched, and it shifts how residents use and defend it. The New York trial also found arrests fell, which suggests deterrence rather than more catching.
    2. The cheapest version can work as well as the expensive one. In Philadelphia, basic clean-up and mowing reduced serious shootings about as much as full greening (Moyer et al., 2019). Maintenance is not the boring principle. It may be the one with the best cost-benefit ratio.
    3. Cameras need people. CCTV did best where someone was actively watching and where it was combined with other measures (Piza et al., 2019). Surveillance is only as good as the response behind it. That is the same point the Layers tool on this site makes about detection and delay.

    Limits and criticisms

    • Eyes on the street are not guardians. Natural surveillance assumes someone is watching and will act. The bystander effect, empty buildings at night and indifferent neighbours all break that link. A place can be “seen” and still unguarded.
    • Context decides. Cul-de-sacs reduce burglary but can concentrate other problems, and permeability helps footfall and natural surveillance in busy centres. The same measure can be right in one place and wrong in another. The EMMIE framework (effect, mechanism, moderators, implementation, economics) used by the UK College of Policing exists because “what works” is always “what works, where, and how”.
    • Fortress and exclusion effects. Gates, fences and hostile architecture can protect one group by pushing others out, and heavy target hardening can raise fear by signalling danger. Good CPTED makes a place feel welcoming to legitimate users and uncomfortable only for offenders.
    • Decay. Lamps fail, hedges grow, gates get propped open. The SBD and lighting studies that show lasting effects are the ones where measures were maintained.
    • The evidence base is uneven. Lighting, vacant-lot remediation, CCTV and gating have strong research behind them. Territoriality and the social strand of CPTED rest mostly on theory and case studies.

    What this means for the model above. The surveillance score counts ground that is both lit and overlooked, which is where the evidence is strongest. Treat it as a measure of opportunity, not of guardianship. A 90% score with nobody behind the windows is not a safe place. Use the findings list to decide what to change first, and the maintenance switch as a reminder that every measure decays.

    A practitioner’s order of work

    1. Start with the crime problem, not the checklist: what happens, where, when, and to whom.
    2. Fix maintenance first. It is cheap, fast, well evidenced and makes everything else work.
    3. Light for people, not for cameras: even coverage on routes and entrances, no glare, no dark pockets beside bright ones.
    4. Open up sightlines from active windows and frontages. Trim planting to below 1 m and lift tree canopies above head height.
    5. Control access where crime concentrates: gate rear alleys and define private space, while keeping the public route legible and welcoming.
    6. Add cameras only with a monitoring and response plan, and combine them with other measures.
    7. Evaluate: count before and after, with a comparison area, and look for displacement and diffusion.
