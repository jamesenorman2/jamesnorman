Terrorism (Protection of Premises) Act 2025

Martyn’s Law readiness check

Named after Martyn Hett, who was killed in the Manchester Arena attack in 2017. The Act asks the people responsible for public venues and events to be ready to protect the public from terrorism. Answer a few questions to see where you stand, then read what it means in practice.

1. Is this a building or site, or an event?
2. What is it wholly or mainly used for?

These are the 17 uses listed in Schedule 1 of the Act. Premises are only in scope if they’re wholly or mainly used for one of them. Uses aimed at “visiting members of the public” still count when entry is limited to ticket holders, paying customers, members or guests.

3. How many people could reasonably be there at the same time?

The Act counts the number of people it is reasonable to expect on the premises at the same time. Think about your busiest realistic moment: a match day, a festival weekend, the Christmas rush, not an average Tuesday.

4. Is entry controlled, for example by tickets, wristbands or checks at the door?

The law in one minute

200–799 people

Standard tier

Tell the regulator, and have simple, practised procedures: evacuation, invacuation, lockdown and how you’ll warn people. It’s about people and planning, not expensive kit.

800+ people, and qualifying events

Enhanced tier

Everything in the standard tier, plus public protection measures that reduce vulnerability, documented and shared with the regulator, with a designated senior individual responsible.

Always standard tier

Four exceptions

Places of worship, childcare, primary and secondary schools, and further education stay in the standard tier however many people attend.

Regulator

The SIA

The Security Industry Authority regulates the duties: giving advice, inspecting, and issuing compliance notices and penalties.

Good to know

Who is the “responsible person”?

Whoever has control of the premises in connection with its qualifying use: usually the operator, occupier or owner running the venue. For an event, it’s whoever has control of the event. Where premises are shared, each person in control should cooperate. Hospitals and higher education institutions have specific rules naming who is responsible.

What do “procedures” and “measures” mean?

Procedures (both tiers) are what your people do if an attack happens or is suspected:

  • Evacuation: getting people out, safely and quickly.
  • Invacuation: bringing people inside, away from danger outside.
  • Lockdown: securing the site to stop an attacker getting in or moving around.
  • Communication: how you warn and instruct staff and the public.

Measures (enhanced tier) reduce how vulnerable the site is in the first place, so far as reasonably practicable. For example, how you monitor the site and its surroundings, manage how people move in and out, and the physical safety and security of the premises and the information about them.

When does it apply?

The Act received Royal Assent in April 2025, followed by an implementation period of at least 24 months for the regulator to be set up and for organisations to prepare. The duties aren’t enforceable until they’re brought into force, so check GOV.UK for the commencement date. Now is the time to get ready.

What happens if you don’t comply?

The SIA can give advice, inspect, and take enforcement action, including compliance notices and penalties.

  • Standard tier: up to £10,000, plus up to £500 a day for continued non-compliance.
  • Enhanced tier: up to £18 million or 5% of qualifying worldwide revenue, whichever is greater, plus up to £50,000 a day.
What should I do now?
  1. Work out who the responsible person is, and confirm your tier with the official guidance.
  2. Write down your evacuation, invacuation, lockdown and communication procedures, and make them fit your actual site.
  3. Train your staff and rehearse. Free training is available from ProtectUK, including Action Counters Terrorism (ACT) e-learning.
  4. Enhanced tier: assess your vulnerabilities, plan proportionate measures, document them, and name your senior individual.
  5. Watch for the SIA’s guidance and the date the duties come into force.

Based on the Act, Schedule 1 and the Home Office statutory guidance. A quick guide, not legal advice. Schedule 2 of the Act lists some exclusions this check doesn’t cover.
